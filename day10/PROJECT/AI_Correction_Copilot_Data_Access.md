# Investigation Course Correction (AI-Correction)

## Initial Assessment
I initially suspected that the reported Copilot behavior meant unauthorized client-data access had already occurred.

## Why I Thought This
That first instinct was understandable because the report came from Legal, involved sensitive client matter context, and carried immediate confidentiality implications.

## Evidence Review
Confirmed findings from the RCA were:
- One user reported seeing client-related matter they believed they should not access.
- The concern was reported at 09:14.
- Related environment changes had recently occurred.
- No validated screenshots, access audits, or verified retrieval evidence were attached.

Assumptions at that stage were:
- Unauthorized access had occurred.
- The output necessarily represented a confirmed restricted retrieval event.

## Evidence That Contradicted My Initial Theory
I had no validated proof that the user lacked effective access at that exact time. I also had no verified evidence of what exact content was shown, where it came from, or whether the event was isolated or systemic.

## Turning Point
The turning point was recognizing that high risk does not equal confirmed breach. The report demanded urgent handling, but evidence still did not support a final access-violation conclusion.

## Revised Assessment
I reframed the case as a potential confidentiality incident requiring immediate containment and formal review, while keeping multiple hypotheses open until entitlement and source validation could be completed.

## Final Conclusion
Final RCA status remained Root Cause Not Yet Confirmed because I had no validated proof of effective access at event time, no verified source-path evidence, and no confirmed scope; the only confirmed conclusion was a credible security signal requiring highest-priority investigation.

## Lesson Learned
I learned to balance urgency with evidentiary discipline: escalate immediately for risk control, but do not state breach conclusions until access facts are verified.

## Reflection Statement
One clear moment where my first instinct was wrong occurred when I assumed the report itself proved unauthorized access. In a Legal context, that reaction is understandable, and rapid escalation was correct. But the evidence did not support a confirmed breach at that point. I did not have validated screenshots, verified source path, or confirmed point-in-time entitlement evidence. That realization changed my approach: I kept the matter at highest priority for containment and investigation, while explicitly avoiding premature conclusions. I shifted from "breach confirmed" thinking to "potential confidentiality incident under formal review." This preserved urgency without overstating facts, protected decision quality, and reduced the risk of both false reassurance and false alarm in leadership communication.
